This is how Nyte's new logo was made. The work ran as one long session in Nyte, on 13 and 14 November 2026. The designer directed. An agent in Nyte drew every version in code and rendered it. No drawing app was used. The geometry lives in Python scripts, even for the Blender scenes, and macOS's own tools made the pictures. The scripts named on this page sit in a local folder that is not part of the repository.

Everything here comes from the notes the agent kept and the files the session left. Where that record is thin, the page says so. Read it top to bottom. Each round shows what was tried, what the designer said and what changed because of it, wrong turns included. The page opens with the brief and two references, and the last sections check the final shape against both.

Where things stand:

- The new icon is in the working tree. It is not committed and not released.
- The motion work is unfinished. Eight patterns exist and none is chosen. Nothing from it is built into the site or the apps.
- Pictures of the icon with glass edges are drawn by Apple's own `ictool` from the Icon Composer document, so they show what macOS draws.

## The brief

The job was to rethink Nyte as a brand and make one macOS app icon, 1024 by 1024 pixels, that reads at a glance as a dark night-time app. The designer asked for:

- A simple, iconic shape built from circles, lines and deliberate ratios, and delicate up close.
- A warm feeling, a child's wonder, and a sense of being made with care.
- Originality. No people and nothing literal.
- Bright or washed colours, and distinct layers, the way a Blender scene has them.

Those are the feelings the rest of the page is checked against. The section called Where the brief landed goes through them one at a time, including the ones that did not land.

## References

Two references shaped the work. Both are other people's, and neither is reproduced here.

The first is the app icons of [Gavin Nelson](https://nelson.co), a designer whose site has an App icons section. The designer likes them, and the session kept a sheet of them to look at. The notes do not say which icons, or which qualities of them, the designer meant.

The second is a thread by Martin Grasser, one of the three designers of Twitter's bird. On 24 July 2023 he [posted how the bird was drawn](https://x.com/martingrasser/status/1683266038602010624). The part that mattered here is the method. The team built their drawings from circles, and he says that gave the bird an underlying neutrality and simplicity. In the session, the designer pointed at the thread when asking for a bunch of circles to be drawn and a pattern to be found in them. That is the circles round below.

## The icon it replaced

The old icon is a 12-cell dithered moon, lit from the upper left. The moon is `#c1d0f6`, on a plate that fades from `#232a44` to `#0c1020`. The notes do not record why the designer wanted to replace it.

The new icon keeps those plate colours. The old moon's blue, `#c1d0f6`, is the pale end of the new moon. This page calls it Nyte blue.

The notes record no designer feedback in this round. Instead, a fresh agent was shown a render with no context and asked what it saw. No transcripts were kept, and the notes do not say how many readers there were. The findings:

- Nested discs read as an eyeball or a fried egg.
- `lid` read as a dark-mode toggle or a Venn diagram.
- `cradle-dusk`, a moon breaking the rim of a larger disc, read best.

What changed: a moon breaking the rim of a larger disc became halo, and halo's circles became the final mark. Lid stayed in play too. The script's own comment on `cradle-dusk` says it goes pale under the moon, so its cut is hard to see, which is the problem the next round names.

## Measure it

<TripFeedback>
  Take it apart and measure it with maths. The contrast at the cut is too low, and there is too much
  gradient.
</TripFeedback>

Everything moved onto Apple's grid, 8 cells across a 1024 pixel tile, so a cell is 128 pixels, with a margin of one cell. Two tools came out of this round.

`measure.py` measures each edge on the picture macOS draws, not on the flat colours, because macOS adds a shadow, a rim light and its own shading. Along an edge it compares a patch inside with a patch outside, as a WCAG contrast ratio. A ratio of 1 means no difference and 21 is black on white. WCAG asks for 3 where a shape has to be made out.

`midway` in `icon_composer.py` computes the tone at the cut so that it sits equally far, by contrast, from the light and from the plate. A third script, `deconstruct.py`, drew a sheet that takes a design apart.

## Shadows

<TripFeedback>
  Play with shadows. The highlighted part is too bright, and the sharp edges have no shadow to give
  a feeling of depth.
</TripFeedback>

The first answer got it backwards. The light floated above the moon, and its shadow was painted on the moon. The designer pointed at the renders and said the light and the main object were reversed.

The fix made the moon the piece in front. Its edge casts a soft shadow onto the light, which makes the moon read as the nearer piece. The two shapes are one piece of glass, and the shadow is a layer painted on top of the light. The day plate was toned down from `#f3f6fe` so that its top edge would not glare.

macOS lets a shadow be made stronger or weaker, but not softer, wider or moved. So the shadow is painted as its own layer, which also means it does not follow macOS's lighting. The sheet shows the shadow at seven settings in both looks, with one dial turned at a time.

## The final design, measured

The source of truth is the variant `wax` in `icon_composer.py`. Every figure below is drawn from numbers that script sets, or from a calculation on them. The drawings are inline SVG.

### The grid

The tile is 1024 pixels, split into 8 cells of 128. The moon has a radius of 3 cells, 384 pixels, and sits at the tile's centre. The light has a radius of 2 cells, 256 pixels, and sits one cell up and one cell right of it. The light breaks the moon's rim by about 53 pixels. Both circles touch the lines one cell in from the tile's top and right edges.

<LogoGrid />

As a mark, in a box of 96 units, one cell is 16 units. The moon is a circle of radius 48 at (48, 48), and the light a circle of radius 32 at (64, 32).

### Two thirds

The two circles are related by one ratio. The light is the moon scaled by two thirds toward the point where the top and right margin lines meet. That point is (7, 1). The moon's centre is 3√2 cells from it and the light's is 2√2, and the radii run 3 and 2.

<LogoScaling />

This is why the two circles touch the same two lines. A scale about a point keeps every tangent line through that point where it was.

### The blue at the cut

The moon is deepest where the light meets it, and `#647fcb` is computed, not picked. It is the tone that stands as far from the cream light as from the plate, by WCAG contrast, at the hue of Nyte blue. Unrounded, it sits 3.65 from the light and 3.65 from the plate's top colour. Once it is rounded to a hex code the two ratios drift to 3.64 and 3.66, which the drawing shows.

The moon then pales toward its far side by a radial gradient centred on the light, in 17 steps from `#647fcb` to Nyte blue.

<LogoCutTone />

### How an edge is read

A reading compares a small patch just inside an edge with one just outside, 26 pixels away, on the picture macOS draws. The patches are 3 by 3 pixels, averaged. Readings are taken every 2 degrees wherever the edge is on view. The notes record the method, and no run of `measure.py` on the final icon.

<LogoEdgeReading />

### One ink

For use inside an interface, the mark can be drawn in a single colour. The two shapes are then told apart by how much of the ink each gets, and the light is always the lighter shape. On a dark ground the moon is drawn at 47% and the light at full strength. On a light ground the moon is full and the light is at 58%.

The two opacities are computed the same way as the moon's deep blue. The function `between` in `logo.py` finds the opacity at which the dimmed shape sits as far, by contrast, from the other shape as from the ground. On a dark ground the moon is the one dimmed, halfway between the light and the ground. On a light ground it is the light, halfway between the moon and the ground. The result is rounded to the nearest hundredth, which is why the two sides of each pair differ a little.

<LogoOneInk />

In the working tree, the design site's header mark follows the same rule, with the site's accent colour as the ink.

### The shadow

The moon's shadow on the light has three numbers. Its strength is 0.55 at the moon's edge. It reaches 0.4 cells across the light at its deepest, which is 51.2 pixels. And it is deepest at 9 o'clock on the light's rim, thinning to nothing toward 12 and 6 o'clock. It is drawn only where the light lies inside the moon's circle, because the part of the light that hangs past the moon has nothing over it.

<LogoShadow />

## Two looks, three finishes

The icon has two looks, drawn from one Icon Composer document.

| Look  | Plate                  | Moon                                         | Light     |
| ----- | ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| Night | `#232a44` to `#0c1020` | `#647fcb` at the light, paling to `#c1d0f6`  | `#fff9d8` |

| --------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

Four things turned up on the way that a reader may not know:

- The whole Icon Composer document is written by a script and drawn with `ictool`, a command-line tool inside Icon Composer's app bundle.
- macOS treats the background as the backdrop. It goes near-black in Dark, clear in Clear and tinted in Tinted. So the night is the background and the moon is a layer. Left the other way round, the moon goes black in Dark.
- A document with a plain fill ignores its per-appearance fills, so every fill is given as a list of appearances.
- macOS's SVG renderer ignores `fill-opacity` on an inherited fill, so the one-ink marks dim with `opacity`.

Four places still show the old dithered moon, on purpose. They are the terminal's welcome animation, the docs landing page's ASCII moons, the docs site's main share image and the spinner.

One decision is open. The Dock icon could be the night look in both modes. It is a one-line change.

## Motion, an open question

This section is a proposal, not shipped work. Eight patterns exist and none is chosen.

`logo-motion.mjs` holds the drawing and the eight timelines. A second script steps them into frames, and a preview page plays them. Each is a 6 second loop that starts and ends on the finished mark. The timelines are GSAP, and only the local, untracked lab package installs GSAP, so the published site cannot import it today. That is why this page has stills and a video, and no live animation.

## What is weak and what is undecided

Known weak points, as the designer was told:

- At 32 pixels and below the shadow disappears, and the icon reads as a dot inside a ring.
- The painted shadow does not follow macOS's lighting.
- The thin bright line on the glass edges is macOS's own highlight. No setting for it was found.
- No blind reader has seen the final design.
- In light mode the icon is not dark, which the brief asked for.

Left undecided or unchecked:

- Which of the eight motion patterns, if any. The motion has not been watched, checked small, or given a reduced-motion form.
- Whether the Dock icon should be the night look in both modes.
- Whether the final design's edges meet the contrast of 3 on the picture macOS draws. `measure.py` was built for the earlier two-disc designs, and the notes record no run on the final.
- How the early blind reads were run. The count of readers is not recorded and no transcripts were kept.
- Why the old icon was replaced. The notes do not say.
- The icon is uncommitted and unreleased. Nothing on this page has shipped.
